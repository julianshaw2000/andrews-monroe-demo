Before you call

What to have to hand

A first call is quicker and cheaper if you can put your hands on these. None of them are essential.

  • The document itself The contract, order, quote or email chain that set the terms — including anything signed years ago and forgotten.
  • How the business is owned Sole trader, partnership or limited company, and whether there is a partnership or shareholders' agreement.
  • The money What is owed or at stake, and whether it has been invoiced. Value shapes what is proportionate to spend.
  • The dates When the agreement was made and when things went wrong. Most contract claims run to a limitation period.

Please keep your first message brief and do not send documents until the firm has confirmed there is no conflict and that it can act for you.

What the firm does

Commercial matters handled here

Most commercial problems arrive in one of two states: something is being set up, or something has broken. The work below covers both, and the same person does the drafting and the arguing — which tends to make the drafting more careful.

Getting the agreement right

  • Terms and conditions of business and sale — payment terms, delivery, liability, and what happens when the scope changes.
  • Partnership and collaboration agreements — who decides what, how money is split, and how someone leaves.
  • Confidentiality and non-disclosure agreements — what is protected, for how long, and what the other side may do with it.
  • Website terms and conditions — the terms your customers actually contract on when they buy online.
  • Contract review — a plain-language reading of what a document someone has sent you really commits you to.

When it has already gone wrong

  • Director and partnership disputes — deadlock, exits, buy-outs and conduct that breaches the agreement.
  • Contract disputes — non-payment, defective performance, termination and what the contract actually permits.
  • Pre-action correspondence — the letter that resolves matters more often than proceedings do.
  • Negotiated settlement — because a long fight between people who have to keep trading rarely serves either of them.

Why the documents matter more than they seem

If you sell anything to anyone, there is already a contract. The only real question is whose terms it is on. Where nothing was agreed in writing, the answer gets decided later, expensively, by reference to emails, conduct and whatever was printed on the back of a purchase order.

The same is true of ownership. Partnership and shareholders' agreements look like paperwork for a problem you do not have — until two people who built something together want different things from it. At that point the document you signed at the start determines almost everything about how quickly and how cheaply it ends.

Common questions

Commercial law, answered plainly

Do I really need written terms and conditions?

If you sell anything to anyone, you already have a contract — the only question is whose terms it is on. Without your own written terms you are trading on whatever was said, implied, or printed on the other side's purchase order, and you will be arguing about payment dates, liability and what happens when the work changes.

Written terms settle those points once, in advance, instead of during a dispute when the relationship has already soured.

What is the difference between a partnership agreement and a shareholders' agreement?

A partnership agreement governs a business run as a partnership. A shareholders' agreement governs the relationship between the owners of a limited company and sits alongside the company's articles of association.

Both do the same underlying job: they say who decides what, how money is shared, and what happens when someone wants to leave or the others want them out. Which one you need depends on how the business is structured, which is worth confirming before anything is drafted.

Is an NDA worth having?

It is worth having before you disclose anything you would mind seeing used against you — a customer list, a price model, a design, a plan to approach a particular market.

An NDA is most useful for what it makes explicit: what counts as confidential, how long the obligation lasts, and what the recipient is allowed to do with the information. A short, clear one signed before the meeting beats a long one signed after it.

My co-director and I have fallen out. What are my options?

Start with the paperwork: the articles of association, any shareholders' or partnership agreement, and the service contracts. Those documents usually decide who can do what, and they are often the reason a dispute resolves quickly or drags on.

Options generally run from a negotiated exit or buy-out, through mediation, to court proceedings. The first conversation is about which of those is realistic in your case and what each would be likely to cost.

Can you review a contract someone else has sent me?

Yes, and it is one of the most common requests. You get a plain-language explanation of what the document actually commits you to, which clauses are unusual or one-sided, and what is worth asking to change.

A discrete piece of advice like this is normally quoted as a fixed fee before the work starts, so you know the number before you commit.
