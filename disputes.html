Three things that decide the case

Before the merits are worth discussing, these usually determine whether a claim is worth bringing at all.

  • The deadline Most contract and negligence claims run to six years; claims on a deed to twelve. Employment tribunal claims run to months, not years.
  • The evidence What can actually be proved, in documents, rather than what both sides remember being said.
  • The proportion What is at stake against what it costs to recover. A modest claim can cost more to run than it returns.
  • And before issuing You are expected to set out your position and consider settlement. Ignoring that can be penalised in costs even if you win.

General information, not advice. Limitation periods vary by claim and some run from a date you may not yet know. If you think a deadline may be close, call rather than email.

What the firm does

Disputes handled here

Litigation is a tool, not a position. The work below is ordered the way a dispute usually is: establish where you stand, put it to the other side properly, and issue proceedings only when that has not worked.

Before proceedings

  • An honest assessment — the merits, the evidence, the limitation position and what recovery would realistically look like.
  • Pre-action correspondence — the letter before action that sets out your case and, more often than not, ends the dispute.
  • Responding to a claim against you — including where the letter you have received overstates its own case.
  • Negotiated settlement — offers, terms, and making sure the agreement actually ends the matter.
  • Mediation — preparing for it, and using it where the parties have to keep dealing with each other afterwards.

Proceedings

  • County Court claims — issue and defence, directions, disclosure, witness evidence and hearings.
  • Tribunal proceedings — including employment tribunal claims, which run to their own much shorter clock.
  • Instructing counsel — where a barrister adds value, with the fee agreed with you before it is incurred.

Typical subject matter

  • Contract and commercial disputes — non-payment, defective performance, and disputed termination.
  • Director and partnership disputes — deadlock, exits and conduct in breach of the agreement.
  • Technology and supplier disputes — failed implementations, licence and scope arguments.
  • Property and contribution claims — including between people who were never married.

Why settlement is the default

Costs risk runs in both directions. The usual rule is that the losing party pays a proportion of the winner's costs — rarely all of them, and always at the court's discretion. On the small claims track recovery is very limited, which means a modest claim can cost more to pursue than it returns even when you are entirely right.

That is not an argument for giving way. It is an argument for knowing the number before you start, and for taking a reasonable offer seriously when it arrives. Where the other side will not engage, proceedings follow — but they follow a decision, rather than substituting for one.

Who the firm acts for

Bringing a claim, or on the receiving end of one

Acting for claimants and defendants means the advice you get has already considered how the other side will answer it — which is usually what makes an early settlement possible.

If you are owed something

Common questions

Disputes, answered plainly

How long do I have to bring a claim?

For most contract and negligence claims the limitation period is six years, running from the breach or from the date the damage occurred. Claims on a document executed as a deed generally run to twelve years.

There are important exceptions, including where the damage was not reasonably discoverable at the time, and employment tribunal claims run to far shorter limits — usually three months less one day. Once a limitation period expires the claim is normally lost however strong it was, so the date is the first thing to establish.

Do I have to try to settle before going to court?

You are expected to. Before issuing proceedings the parties are generally required to set out their positions in correspondence, exchange key documents and genuinely consider alternatives such as mediation.

This is not a formality. A party that ignores it, or refuses to engage with settlement without good reason, can be penalised in costs even if it goes on to win the case.

What is a letter before action, and does it work?

It is the letter that sets out what you say happened, what you want and by when — written so that it would read well to a judge later.

It is also the single most cost-effective step in most disputes. A properly evidenced letter frequently produces payment or a serious offer without proceedings, because it is the point at which the other side has to take a view on their own risk.

If I win, does the other side pay my legal costs?

Usually the losing party pays a proportion of the winner's costs — but rarely all of them, and always at the court's discretion. On the small claims track costs recovery is very limited, which means a modest claim can cost more to run than it returns.

Costs risk runs both ways. It is one of the main reasons settlement is worth taking seriously before proceedings rather than after.

Is mediation worth doing?

Often, and particularly where the parties have to keep dealing with each other afterwards — business partners, suppliers, neighbours. Mediation is confidential and without prejudice, so an offer made there cannot be used against you if it fails.

It also tends to settle matters that correspondence alone will not, because both sides hear the risks in the same room on the same day.

What does a dispute cost to run?

The standard hourly rate is £450. Court fees and other disbursements are billed on top and can be substantial in themselves; large disbursements are payable in advance, and any barrister's fee — commonly £1,500 to £5,000 a day — is agreed with you before it is incurred. The firm is not VAT registered, so no VAT is added to its fees, though disbursements may carry VAT charged by the supplier. Early stages such as a letter before action are often quoted as a fixed fee, commonly between £600 and £1,700.

You will be told at the outset what the realistic cost is against what is at stake — including when the answer is that a claim is not worth bringing. Full price transparency.
