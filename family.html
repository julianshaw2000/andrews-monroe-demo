What to expect

How a divorce actually runs

Two waiting periods are built into the process. Neither can be shortened, so it is worth knowing them at the start.

  • Step one · the application Made by one of you or jointly. No fault, no reasons, and no realistic way to contest it.
  • Then a minimum of 20 weeks Before the conditional order. This is the period the law sets aside for reflection and for sorting out the finances.
  • Then 6 weeks and a day Before the final order can be applied for. A floor of roughly six months, even when everything is agreed.
  • Separately · the money The divorce does not resolve finances. That needs its own order, and it usually sets the real timetable.

General information, not advice. If you or your children are at immediate risk, call 999. Urgent protective orders exist and do not wait for any of the above.

What the firm does

Family matters handled here

Family work is rarely only about law. It is about a house, a pension, and where the children spend Christmas — usually at a point when nobody is at their best. The work below is done with that in mind, and with settlement as the default rather than the fallback.

Divorce and dissolution

  • The divorce or dissolution itself — the application, the two waiting periods, and the conditional and final orders.
  • Separation without divorce — where you are not ready to end the marriage, or have reasons not to.
  • Advice on timing — including why finalising the divorce before the finances are resolved is usually a mistake.

Money and property

  • Financial settlements — the house, savings, pensions, income and debts, and what a realistic outcome looks like.
  • Consent orders — turning an agreement into something the court approves and that actually ends the claims.
  • Contested financial proceedings — disclosure, negotiation and hearings where agreement is not reached.
  • Property disputes — including between people who were never married.

Children

  • Arrangements for children — where they live, when they see each parent, and how that changes as they grow.
  • Specific decisions — schooling, medical treatment, names and travel, where parents cannot agree.
  • Relocation and international disputes — one parent wanting to move away, within the UK or abroad.

If you were never married

  • Property and contribution claims — whose name is on the title, what was paid, and what was agreed.
  • Claims for the benefit of children — available to parents regardless of marital status.
  • Cohabitation agreements — recording what you both intend, while you still agree on it.

Two things worth knowing before you start

The first is that the divorce and the money are separate. Ending the marriage does not end the financial claims between you. Without a financial order approved by the court, those claims can stay open for years — including against money or property acquired long after you separated. It is the most expensive misunderstanding in family law, and it is entirely avoidable.

The second is that there is no such thing as common law marriage. Living together, however long, creates no automatic entitlement to the other person's property, pension or income. If that is your situation, what you can establish about contributions and intentions matters far more than it would in a divorce.

Common questions

Family law, answered plainly

Do I have to prove my spouse did something wrong to get divorced?

No. Since April 2022, divorce and dissolution in England and Wales are no-fault. You state that the marriage or civil partnership has broken down irretrievably, and that statement is accepted — there is no requirement to give reasons and no realistic way for the other person to contest it. Applications can be made by one of you or jointly.

Taking blame out of the process tends to make the parts that actually matter — the finances and the children — easier to settle.

How long does a divorce take?

The process has two built-in waiting periods: a minimum of 20 weeks between the application and the conditional order, and a further 6 weeks and a day before the final order can be applied for. That sets a floor of roughly six months even where everything is agreed.

In practice it is the financial settlement, not the divorce, that determines the real timetable — and it is usually unwise to finalise the divorce before the finances are resolved.

Does the divorce sort out the money as well?

No, and this is the most costly misunderstanding in family law. Ending the marriage and resolving the finances are separate processes.

Without a financial order approved by the court, financial claims between you can remain open for years, including claims against money or property acquired long after you separated. Where matters are agreed, that agreement is normally recorded in a consent order and sent to the court for approval.

Do we have to go to court about the children?

Usually not, and the courts actively prefer that you do not. Most arrangements for children are agreed directly or through mediation, and before making most court applications you are normally required to attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting to consider the alternatives.

Exemptions apply, including in cases involving domestic abuse. Court remains available where agreement is not possible, or not safe.

We were never married. What am I entitled to?

There is no such thing as common law marriage in England and Wales, however long you have lived together. Unmarried partners have no automatic claim on each other's property, pensions or income on separation.

Claims usually turn instead on property law — whose name is on the title, what was contributed, and what was agreed or promised — which makes evidence of contributions and intentions unusually important. Parents may also bring claims for the benefit of their children.
