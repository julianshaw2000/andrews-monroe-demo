Know first

The data breach clock

If personal data has been exposed, the reporting window is short and it starts before you have the full picture.

  • Within 72 hours Notify the ICO of a notifiable personal data breach, from becoming aware of it — not from finishing the investigation.
  • Without undue delay Tell the affected people too, where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms.
  • Every time, either way Record the incident and your reasoning, including where you decide it is not notifiable.
  • Before any of that Know who your controller and processor are, and what your contracts already commit you to. That decides who reports.

General information, not advice, and not every incident is notifiable. If something has happened, call rather than email — the clock is already running.

What the firm does

Technology matters handled here

Technology work fails in predictable places: who owns the code, what happens when the project overruns, and what the contract says about data. The work below is aimed at those points, before they become the argument.

Contracts and licensing

  • Software licences and IT contracts — scope, acceptance, support obligations and what happens at the end of the term.
  • Development and consultancy agreements — deliverables, change control, payment milestones and ownership of what gets built.
  • Reseller, referral and collaboration agreements — who may sell what, to whom, and on whose terms.
  • Outsourcing arrangements — where part of the service you provide, or rely on, is delivered by somebody else.
  • Reviewing the other side's contract — a plain reading of the licence or master services agreement you have been asked to sign.

Data protection

  • UK GDPR compliance — lawful basis, retention, individual rights and what you actually have to document.
  • Data processing agreements — the written terms required between a controller and its processor.
  • Privacy and cookie policies — written to match how the site really works, not a template.
  • Security and breach response — deciding whether an incident is notifiable, and recording the decision.
  • ICO registration — whether you need to be registered and pay the data protection fee.

Websites, eCommerce and IP

  • Website terms and conditions — the terms your users and customers are actually contracting on.
  • Selling online to consumers — pre-contract information, the order process, and cancellation rights.
  • Intellectual property protection — copyright in code and design, confidential know-how, and assignments that make ownership explicit.

The clause worth checking before anything else

By default, the developer who writes the code usually owns the copyright in it — even where the client paid for the work. Ownership moves to the client only if the contract says so, in writing. That gap sits quietly in a great many development agreements and surfaces at the worst possible moment: when the relationship ends and the client wants to take the code to someone else.

Whichever side of that you are on, it is cheap to fix at the start and expensive to argue about later. The same is true of acceptance criteria — if nobody wrote down what "finished" means, both parties are relying on the other being reasonable about it.

Common questions

Technology law, answered plainly

How quickly must a personal data breach be reported to the ICO?

Where a personal data breach is likely to result in a risk to people's rights and freedoms, a controller must notify the Information Commissioner's Office without undue delay and, where feasible, no later than 72 hours after becoming aware of it. Where the risk to individuals is high, the affected people usually have to be told as well.

Not every incident is notifiable, and the decision has to be recorded either way. That is why the assessment is worth working out before anything happens rather than during it.

Who owns the code once a development project is finished?

By default, the developer who wrote the code usually owns the copyright in it, even where the client paid for the work. Ownership passes to the client only if the contract says so in writing.

This is the single most common gap in development agreements, and it surfaces at the worst possible time — when the relationship ends and the client wants to take the code elsewhere. It is fixed by an assignment or a properly drafted licence, agreed at the start.

Do I need a cookie banner on my website?

If your site sets cookies or similar technologies that are not strictly necessary to deliver the service the visitor asked for — analytics, advertising, most embedded third-party content — you generally need the visitor's consent before setting them, under the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations. Strictly necessary cookies do not need consent.

A banner that sets analytics cookies before anyone has clicked anything does not achieve compliance, whatever the banner says. This site, for what it is worth, sets none at all.

What is the difference between a data controller and a data processor?

A controller decides why and how personal data is processed. A processor acts on the controller's instructions. Most agencies, hosts and SaaS suppliers are processors for their clients' data and controllers for their own.

It matters because the obligations differ, and because a written contract between controller and processor containing specific required terms is a legal requirement rather than good practice.

Are my website terms and conditions enough for selling online?

Selling to consumers online brings requirements beyond ordinary contract terms: pre-contract information, an order process that makes the obligation to pay clear, and cancellation rights that consumers cannot sign away.

Terms copied from another site rarely match the way your business actually sells. It is the inconsistencies between your terms, your checkout and your refund practice that cause the arguments.
